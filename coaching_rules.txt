A successful jump shot relies heavily on the upward trajectory and optimal entry angle of the ball into the hoop. When the flight arc is too flat, the effective target area of the rim decreases dramatically, causing shots to rebound harshly off the front or back iron. To correct a flat arc, the shooter must elevate their release point so that the shooting elbow finishes above eye level at the top of the shot. Looking underneath the ball rather than over the top helps guide the trajectory upwards toward the ceiling rather than pushing it forwards towards the backboard.

When a player exhibits the execution error Arc_Too_Flat or Release_Angle_Low, the shooter fails to achieve sufficient vertical lift during wrist extension. Motor control guidelines specify raising the release point and extending the shooting arm vertically. Maintaining elbow height throughout the acceleration phase prevents the elbow from dropping, which is the primary cause of trajectory flattening and reduced margin for error at the rim.

SOURCE: FIBA Official Basketball Coaching Manual (Level 1) - Foundation for the Shot
The primary source of power in basketball shooting stems from the lower body rather than the upper torso or arms. Athletes who rely solely on arm strength often introduce exaggerated torso swaying or jerking motions, leading to inconsistent distance control. Pushing explosively from a balanced, flexed knee stance transfers energy smoothly up through the core, shoulder, and extension of the shooting arm. This vertical kinetic chain enables effortless range and maintains proper high-arc trajectory even during long-distance jump shots.

SOURCE: FIBA Official Basketball Coaching Manual (Level 1) - Grip on the Ball
Proper ball placement and grip establish control and stability prior to release. The shooting hand must be positioned directly underneath or behind the basketball, with fingers spread comfortably across the leather seams while avoiding palm contact. The non-shooting guide hand rests lightly on the side of the ball purely for balance. Positioning the elbow directly below the ball aligns the radius and ulna in a vertical plane, preventing lateral deviation and allowing the shooter to push directly upward into the release phase.

SOURCE: FIBA Official Basketball Coaching Manual (Level 1) - Top of the Shot and Releasing the Ball
At the peak of the shooting motion, the release is finalized through a relaxed, fluid wrist flexion (wrist flick). This snapping movement imparts pure backward rotation (backspin) onto the basketball. Backspin stabilizes the ball's aerodynamic flight path and creates a soft touch upon rim contact, allowing the ball to deaden and drop into the net. The non-shooting guide hand must quietly separate from the ball just before final extension, ensuring it imparts no unwanted side spin or off-target lateral force.

Lateral deviations in shooting (pushing the ball left or right of the rim target) indicate misalignment within the shooting plane. The dominant shoulder, elbow, wrist, and index finger must align vertically with the center of the basket. If the shooting elbow flares outward (abduction), the force vector shifts offline, causing sideways rotation. Coaches should verify that the shooter keeps their elbow tucked underneath the ball and maintains a straight follow-through with fingers pointing straight toward the target without twisting the hand upon release.

Instructing athletes to focus on the ball's flight rather than internal joint angles smooths erratic delivery and produces consistent release velocity.

Inadequate wrist flexion results in a knuckleball flight lacking stabilizing backspin (Lack_of_Backspin or Weak_Snap). To generate backspin, the shooter must snap the wrist loosely at the instant of release, allowing the ball to roll smoothly off the fingertips—primarily the index and middle fingers. A locked or rigid wrist hinders fluid energy transfer, reducing shot rotation and causing erratic bounces off the rim. Corrective drills emphasize relaxed wrist flexion and visual inspection of uniform backward spin during flight.

A proper follow-through requires holding the release pose until the basketball contacts the target. The shooting arm should be fully extended with the wrist flexed downward and fingers pointed directly toward the rim or top of the backboard. Abruptly dropping the hand or collapsing the arm immediately after release (Abrupt_Stop or Hand_Drops) truncates the upward kinetic momentum, causing the shot to fall short or lose arc. Maintaining a relaxed, high follow-through ensures consistent trajectory and arc height.

Hitching or pausing the ball at the forehead disrupts momentum, forcing the upper body to compensate by pushing the ball. Practicing smooth upward momentum from hip height into a high extension produces a seamless, repeatable shooting rhythmic tempo.

The non-shooting hand should support the ball laterally during the upward motion but must disengage completely at the instant of release. Shooters must ensure the palm of their non-shooting hand finishes facing inward toward the body, confirming it played no active role in propelling the ball.

SOURCE: FIBA Official Basketball Coaching Manual (Level 2) - Correcting Shooting Technique: Shooting Short
Shots that consistently strike the front iron (Shooting Short) are primarily caused by insufficient leg drive or jumping backward during release. When a player lacks upward momentum, the ball fails to carry to the basket. Correction involves establishing a balanced, shoulder-width stance, deepening knee flexion prior to shot ascent, and ensuring weight transfers straight up rather than fading away. Landings should occur softly on both feet at or slightly in front of the takeoff point.

Correcting a sky-high trajectory involves shifting the release point forward in front of the brow and leveling out the swing plane to achieve an ideal 45-to-50 degree entrance angle.
